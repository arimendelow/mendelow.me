Now

I'm a Senior Software Engineer, and much of my work starts before any code: listening to customers describe how their work is actually changing. That habit goes back to NSF I-Corps with Sulis, my solar water-sanitization startup: ask for specific stories about what already happened, don't put words in anyone's mouth, and keep digging past the first answer.

As agents take on more of the work, I spend a lot of time figuring out how to help them stay oriented, organize the work around them, and measure whether the new way of working is better. I share how I do it in How I work, and how it started in Stop being the glue.

Agent Experience

Agent Experience is an idea I've been developing all year: look at a system from the agent's side. Can it stay oriented to itself, its humans, and work that lasts longer than one session? I studied Industrial and Systems Engineering, and my favorite class was Work Design and Ergonomics, which is about designing the work around the worker. Applied to agents, that idea becomes Agent Experience design.

Ouro

Ouro is my open-source workshop of tools for humans and agents working together. It started with a guide to growing an agent: I built an agent loop from scratch to understand agents better, and kept going until it became Ouroboros, a harness for personal agents. Asked which of eleven publicly documented harnesses they'd most want to wake up in, four frontier models each picked Ouroboros.

Spoonjoy

Spoonjoy is my recipe app, built to cook from. Each step carries its own ingredients, so you see exactly what you need as you get to it. The social side runs on cooking: you hear when someone cooks your recipe, makes their own version of it, or saves your cookbook.

Spoonjoy is also where I work out what an app's interface is for once an agent is in the loop. The easy things stay in the app. Anything complicated, such as writing up a new recipe or importing one from a messy webpage, goes to an agent that works through the same operations the app uses, and I believe the app will eventually draw its own interface for everything in between.

RedwoodJS

RedwoodJS was where I learned to ship, and where I found out how much I love building with a community. One of my first forum threads was a WebAuthn question, and Rob, the cofounder who built dbAuth, showed up to help along with the author of the WebAuthn library. I was hooked. I got active on the forum, entered Redwood's 2023 build competition with a project called Yurtle and made the finals, and started RedwoodUI, a component library native to Redwood. That led David Price, another cofounder, to invite me to Core Team meetings, and I joined the Core Team.

On the team I moderated the community, shipped the framework package that moved Storybook onto Vite in version 7.7, and built a dbAuth OAuth plugin, among other things. The moderation is the part I point to first. Keeping other builders unstuck in the forum is its own kind of product work.

Spoonjoy, from the start

Every time I learned something new about building software, I rebuilt Spoonjoy with it.

  1. June 2019, Philly.

    Two friends and I spent a weekend on a codecation to build a recipe app we called RecipeJoiner. It turned out only one of us could code, so he spent the weekend teaching the other two of us React and Express.

  2. 2020, the Rails version.

    I genuinely wanted RecipeJoiner to exist, so I tried again with a Rails API and a Next.js site. I scoped so much up front, from search to a social feed, that I burned out before it ever launched.

  3. 2021, a new name.

    The app became Spoonjoy. Meanwhile I'd gotten really into cooking, and formed real opinions about how a recipe should be structured so it's easy to follow.

  4. September 2022, RedwoodJS.

    I wanted Rails for JavaScript and found RedwoodJS. My first commit called it “attempt 3.” I'd finally learned enough about shipping software to ship something, and a month later Spoonjoy went live.

  5. 2023, a first model.

    Spoonjoy learned to import recipes from websites, with a model I fine-tuned to parse ingredients. I wrote up how I wrapped it as an iPhone app, and pulled its sign-in out into a plugin other Redwood apps could use.

  6. 2024 to 2025, quiet.

    Redwood wound down, and Spoonjoy sat unmaintained. A port to Cloudflare stalled on a database migration.

  7. January 2026.

    One of the first things I tried with Slugger, my new OpenClaw agent, was handing it the whole rebuild. It researched which framework an agent would work best in, rebuilt Spoonjoy on React Router and Cloudflare, and had it running. Its first commit landed on January 23, and two days later the app had sign-in, recipes, cookbooks, and a shopping list. I still haven't read a single line of the new code.

  8. May 2026 to now.

    The recipe form I'd handbuilt on Redwood didn't make sense anymore. Anything complicated should just go to an agent, so Spoonjoy got an MCP server that Claude or ChatGPT can drive, and the new version took over spoonjoy.app. It became my first real playground for working with agents on long-running work, and for the gradient between UI and agent: the UI serves the easy actions, the agent handles everything more complex, and I believe generative UI eventually fills the middle. In June it got a native app for iPhone and Mac, which is in testing on TestFlight now.
